I've been having a lot of conversations and coffees recently.  In this economy, everyone is understandably worried about their bottom line (I mean, of course this is true of every economy.  But it seems to be even more so now).  As such, concerns about churn are on the rise, along with many of the churn numbers.  I wanted to share some lessons from the conversations - I think some will be familiar, and others surprising.

## Churn is just the end

Churn is the tail end of what is frequently a long process.  It's not like a customer wakes up one day and goes "nah" and churns.  Things have happened.  There is a story to uncover there. Churn is not an acute disease - it is a chronic one, which means progression over time.

Your typical accounts start healthy, or at least you should assume they start healthy (If they don't, work on your onboarding already!).  They then progress over a series of stages.  Maybe a support ticket goes ignored.  Maybe they work around a bug, then a fix breaks their workflow.  Maybe they get discordant messages from sales and marketing together.

The next step is something like "unhealthy".  They aren't actively eyeing the door, but trust has been damaged.  They might even recommend the product to others, but grudgingly.  A great example of this is actually me, with Riverside!  I frequently say it's my least favorite tech that I always recommend.  I'd love something better and I'm dissatisfied, but I have reason to stay (more on this next!)

Next up is "considering churn".  This is the point of resignation, where they pull back.  Usage may drop, as they move energy and effort elsewhere.  This is a stage where support tickets tend to drop as well.  They may not have churned yet, but they are actively considering it.

Last is "planning churn", or pre-churn.  They've churned in all but paperwork, and the writing is on the wall.  Usage drops farther, as do support tickets.  Success meetings get ghosted, and sales calls are ignored.  The writing is on the wall.

Obviously beyond the final step is where they actively churn and cease to be a customer.  At each step, winback is still possible (we've all seen life pumped back into a logo, or a logo that returns after churning).  But taking a logo from unhealthy to healthy is a lot easier than bringing a pre-churn logo back to just considering churn - and they frequently slide back.

Prevention is the best medicine, where you can afford it.

## Low Churn can be misleading

I was stricken by one particular story about low churn.  In this case churn was down to low single digits, and it wasn't a success story.

In this story, the market was lucrative but small.  For this particular enterprise, the market was heavily occupied, with high switching costs.  Most of the revenue opportunity was through expansion within individual accounts.  However, for this founder, churn was low, but so was expansion.  Their customer base was as cold as ice - each deal essentially frozen in place.

Why? Dissatisfied customers.  Due to the high switching costs and long contract length, all the customers would drop to pre-churn, but wait to churn until the next contract came up.  They would long for a better product, and eventually go to the competition, and expand with the competition to boot.

In essence, the low churn was a lie.  They had already churned - the business just didn't realize it yet.

## You Know.

Controversial take, but I don't really believe in customers churning for reasons outside my control.  I know. I know! It is real, and exists.

But what I mean is: if a single customer churns, it can be due to a wide variety of factors outside my control.  When multiple customers churn, there's frequently an issue on my side (or at least, it can be profitable to assume there is). A point is a point, many points is a line and a trend.  I don't like trends that lose me money.

As we discussed earlier, businesses don't really just churn.  If they're happy and satisfied and seeing ROI, it would be weird to leave.  Churn would be uneconomic, illogical, and frankly painful - and your typical human hates pain.  So if customers are churning they are unhappy, dissatisfied, or not seeing ROI.  These are frequently issues you can act on: IF you know there is a problem.

I've definitely seen people say "I had no idea they were ready to churn!".  On the other hand, in these cases, when I ask around, someone knew.  Usually they knew, and told someone, but it wasn't a priority.  These things have a way of suddenly and aggressively becoming priorities, when you least expect and want them.

The hard work lies in the preventative care, and that lies all through the organization - from marketing to sales to success to support to product to engineering to QA and elsewhere.  You can't control everything, but you can control how you respond - and building the systems to see churn in advance helps you respond in the right way.
